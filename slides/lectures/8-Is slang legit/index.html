How does context matter?

  • Scenario: Dinner table conversation
    • Q1 – Is there a state income tax in Connecticut?
      • R1 – Yes, No, or I don’t know
      • Simply informational
    • Q2 – Is there any salt on the table?
      • R2 – Yes, No, I don’t know…or passing the salt!
      • Not meant to be information request
      • First three responses are rude!

Pragmatics

  • Pragmatics is the study of language use, in particular, the relationship among syntax, semantics, and interpretation in light of the context of the discourse
    • Discourse = sequence of language, joined in situation
    • Context = where we are, what came before, etc.
    • Differs from semantics in that the focus there is on meanings of words, sentences, and phrases in isolation , without regard to contextual information

True or false: Relevance is one of the four main assumptions that pragmatists talk about when it comes to communication. True

  • We say that native speakers have two things:
    • Grammatical competence – the ability to produce and assign meaning to grammatical sentences
      • Our unconscious, or implicit, knowledge of vocabulary, pronunciation, sentence structure, and meaning.
      • Everything a native speaker says = grammatical
    • AND
    • Communicative competence – the ability to produce and interpret utterances relevant to their context of use
      • Our ability to use language appropriately in each context to achieve the function that we intend to achieve
      • This is the part we’ll be focusing on here!

Patterns of use

Contexts of use

  • Let’s consider the example from the video:
  • “Can you close the window?”
    • Interrogative sentence = a request for information (that is, “Are you able to close the window?”)
    • But, in a specific context, say, one where two people are trying to study in a room with an open window, but the marching band is practicing on the field next door…
      • It serves as a request for action
      • The speaker wants the addressee to shut the window
      • Expected response ≠ “Yes”, “No”, “I don’t know”

Real world contexts also call for…

  • Sociolinguistics is the study of the interaction of language and social factors
    • It’s a two-way street – language influences society and society influences language!
    • Language doesn’t exist in a vacuum
  • Plus, NO ONE speaks the same way ALL THE TIME
    • They vary by age, gender, race, situation, region, style, etc.
    • So…context matters!

Why do people vary their speech?

  • People have at their disposal an entire linguistic repertoire
    • The set of language varieties exhibited in the speaking and writing patterns of a speech community
    • We adjust to the speech situation = who, what, where, when, why, how, etc.
    • Remember communicative competence!
  • Languages (and dialects, styles, registers, etc.) can differ in vocabulary, phonology, grammar, semantics, etc. across a variety of speech situations
  • We’ll focus on lexical and semantic markers of differences in speech situations ::: {.notes}

Purpose = The kind of activity taking place and the interlocutors’ goals guide language choice Setting = Changes in topic, location, and mode of communication (speaking vs. writing) guide language choice Participants = Identities of the speaker and addressee and their roles in the interaction guide language choice

Lexical Markers

Slang

  • Contrary to popular belief, slang ≠ bad speech
  • Slang is the register used in situations of extreme informality
    • Its use may signal rebellious undertones or an intentional distancing of its users from certain mainstream values
    • But it may also signal cultural knowledge, intimacy, belonging
    • Everyone can use slang, given the appropriate situation
  • Slang ≠ jargon ::: {.notes}

Jargon is a specialized vocabulary associated with professions (such as medicine, finance, and engineering) and with activities (such as sports, music, and computing) Unlike slang, jargon isn’t limited to situations of extreme informality and doesn’t carry rebellious undertones Words often have meanings other than those in the profession/activity Example Baseball: plate, pitch, curveball, changeup, swing, hit, run, out, homerun, closer, reliever, doubled, singled, homered, etc.
