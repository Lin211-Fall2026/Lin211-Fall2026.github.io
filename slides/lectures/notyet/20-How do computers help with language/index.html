2. Word order is important

  • It affects grammaticality and meaning.
    • Mary dumped John. ≠ John dumped Mary.
    • *John Mary dumped.
    • *Dumped Mary John.
  • Languages differ in word order ::: {.notes}

Word order is more flexible in some languages compared to English, particularly languages that heavily inflect for case.

:::

Word Order

  • We usually talk about word order in terms of grammatical roles like subject (S), object (O), and verb (V)
  • These pieces are combined in different ways
    • SVO, SOV, VSO, VOS, OSV, OVS
    • For example, Irish and Welsh are VSO
    • Siaradodd Aled y Gymraeg.
      • spoke Aled DEF Welsh
      • Verb Subject Object
      • “Aled spoke Welsh.”
  • Almost no languages use OSV or OVS
    • However, every language has some rules about word order.

3. Words form constituents

Give them pro-form substitution as the example of how you know

Constituency

  • We are trying to show that certain words clump together into groups – and even these groups clump together into bigger and bigger groups.
  • So, we have to think about grammar as being hierarchical
    • Like a tree rather than like a line

4. Constituents form a hierarchical structure

  • Each node is equivalent to a constituent!
    • Everything that hangs below a node (directly or indirectly) is inside that particular constituent, and nothing else.
    • One word can belong to several different (bigger and bigger) constituents at different levels.

Why hierarchical?

  • All utterances are said or written in a linear mode.
  • This can lead to ambiguity…
    • Yoko Ono will talk about her husband John Lennon who was killed in an interview with Barbara Walters.
    • How is this ambiguous?

Ambiguity

  • The sentence has 2 meanings:
    • a) Sarah saw [a man with a telescope]. = the man was holding a telescope
    • b) Sarah saw [a man] [with a telescope]. = she used a telescope to see him
  • Diagramming the sentences’ underlying hierarchical structure can disambiguate the meaning.
  • In the scene from 2001: A Space Odyssey, HAL says, “I’m sorry, Dave. I’m afraid I can’t do that”
    • This requires a level of understanding of “Open the pod bay doors, HAL.” that computers just don’t have!
    • All the stuff we’ve said about syntax today is stuff you do automatically!
    • To be able to parse, or break down a sentence into its component parts so that the meaning of the sentence can be understood, a computer would need to (at least) know:
      • Meanings of the words and word order
      • What makes a sentence (specifically an imperative, or command, one)
      • How to respond to imperatives (that they have never encountered and are not likely in the database)
