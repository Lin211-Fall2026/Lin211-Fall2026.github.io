What does it mean to “know” a word?

  • Using/Knowing a word requires (at least) four __ __ kinds of information:
    • Phonetic / phonological information
    • Syntactic information
    • Morphological information
    • Semantic information

What is phonetics/phonology?

  • Phonetics is the study of the sounds in a language
  • Two branches:
    • Articulatory phonetics focuses on the human vocal apparatus and describes sounds in terms of their articulation in the vocal tract
    • Acoustic phonetics uses the tools of physics to study the nature of sound waves produced in human language
  • Phonology is the study of the system of contrastive relationships among the speech sounds
    • It’s about syllables, stress, phonotactics, etc.
    • Often discussed as what is in your mind (which might be different than what comes out of your mouth)

What is syntax?

  • “The way in which words are put together to form phrases and sentences.” (American Heritage Dictionary)
    • It’s all about structure and rules !
    • It is easy to see that some words have some things in common; they share certain characteristics and behave similarly.
  • But in order to talk about structure, we need to know a little about…

Syntactic information

  • Parts of Speech are categories of words based on similar meanings, forms, and functions
    • AKA lexical categories , word classes , syntactic categories
    • Major classes: Nouns, Verbs, Adjectives, Adverbs
    • Minor classes: Pronouns, Determiners, Prepositions, Auxiliaries, Conjunctions
  • Sometimes classification can be fuzzy
    • Same form ≠ same word (ex. Use “e-mail” in a sentence)
    • What’s going on here?

Morphology

  • To explore morphological knowledge, we need the subfield of morphology , which is the study of word formation
  • In morphology, we study a word’s component parts and internal structures
    • How many parts are there in a word?
    • For example, how many parts are in the word friendliness ?
    • These parts are called morphemes – the smallest meaningful unit in language (that is, the lowest level a word may be broken down into and still express some meaning)

Morphological Rules

  • As well as the operations that create and modify those parts and internal structures in various ways, which we call morphological rules
  • Remember, grammar = rules, and all languages have rules!
  • Example rule in English:
    • How do we make a noun into an adjective?
      • Smell + -y = smelly
      • Cat + -like = catlike
      • Man + -ly = manly
  • Morphological rules thus create new words!
